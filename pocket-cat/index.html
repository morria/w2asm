Pocket Cat is a matchbox-sized Bluetooth bridge that plugs into your transceiver's USB CAT port. Frequency, mode, power, memories, even a live panadapter — all from an iPhone in your pocket, no cables to the operator.

What you can do with it

The bridge is a transparent pipe; all the radio smarts live in a native iOS app. That means a real, typed, fast interface — not a terminal.

Tune & operate

Frequency, mode, RF power, and PTT with a dead-man failsafe. Drag the digits to spin the VFO; tap to key in a frequency in kHz.

Live panadapter

On the QMX, stream raw I/Q over the link and watch a real-time spectrum and waterfall on the phone — band activity at a glance.

Every menu, explained

Browse the radio's whole configuration tree with a plain-English explanation for each item — discovered live, so it always matches your firmware.

Save & restore setups

Capture the radio's full state to a profile in iCloud Drive, diff it against the live rig, and apply it back — your Field Day config in one tap.

Truly portable

Battery-powered and pocket-sized. Sit across the room from a hot rig, or run it off a QRP setup in the field — no laptop, no wires to you.

Safe by design

The link can key a transmitter, so it requires an encrypted, bonded pairing, and drops PTT automatically if the connection goes quiet.

How it works

A dumb pipe by design. The bridge shuttles bytes; the phone owns every radio decision — so a new radio is a Swift change, not a firmware one.

Because frequency, band, and mode live only in the app, the same firmware drives any radio — and a new one never touches the C code.

Build your own

A complete unit is about a dozen parts, ~23 g of filament, and an afternoon. Everything is open source — firmware, iOS apps, and the printable case.

You'll need: a 3D printer (PETG), a temperature-controlled soldering iron, a multimeter, and a Mac with Xcode for the app. The full walkthrough — with checkpoints at every stage — lives in BUILD.md.

Also needed, but not part of the unit: a USB-to-UART dongle for flashing. The native USB-C port runs in host mode for the radio, so the console and bootloader live on UART0 — easy to forget when ordering.

Wiring diagram

Seven runs. The switch sits upstream of both the board and the boost, so switching off also kills the boost — that keeps its 5 V from back-feeding a PC's USB when you plug in to reflash.

Battery + / switched power Boost 5 V → VUSB Ground

Two things that will bite you. Cut the LiPo's JST plug off one lead at a time — cutting both together shorts the cell through your cutters, and a 500 mAh pouch will happily weld them. And measure the boost output before it touches the microcontroller: switch on, confirm it reads 5 V on a meter, and only then solder it to VUSB.

Step by step

1

Print the case

Two parts, no supports — pocket_cat_base.stl and pocket_cat_lid.stl. PETG, 0.2 mm layers, 4 walls, 25 % infill. Both are pre-oriented; slice as exported.

The lid's skirt slides inside the base walls.
2

Flash the firmware — before assembly

The programming pads face into the case once it's built, so flash first. Wire a USB-UART dongle to GPIO43/44, then idf.py set-target esp32s3 && idf.py flash. The firmware and every test build with no hardware.

It advertises as CATBridge-XXXX in a BLE scanner.
3

Solder the wiring

Seven runs, ~60 mm each, twisted in pairs (loop area is what radiates into the receiver). Follow the diagram above — cell to switch, switch to the BAT pads and boost input, boost 5 V to VUSB. Hot-glue the BAT joints; those pads lift.

Switch on: the LED lights and it advertises. Switch off: all dark.
4

Assemble the case

Antenna into its bay, switch on its pegs, board into the lid pocket connector-first, boost into its cradle. Lay the cell in loose — never glue it, and leave the 1 mm swelling gap clear. Close with the M3 from underneath, snug only.

Nothing protrudes; the USB-C port is centred in its aperture.
5

Build & install the app

Pick the app for your radio, set your Apple signing team, and run to your iPhone: xcodegen generate, then build in Xcode or from the command line. A built-in simulator runs the whole app with no hardware, so you can try it before the parts even arrive.

The app launches and finds "Simulated" in the connection sheet.
6

First contact

Plug the bridge into the radio's CAT port, power both on, and open the app. It pairs, probes the baud rate, identifies the rig, and goes live — frequency and mode on screen a moment later.

Turn the dial on the app — the radio follows.
