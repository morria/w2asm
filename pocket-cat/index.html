Pocket Cat

A Bluetooth bridge for your HF radio's CAT port

About

  • A matchbox-sized, battery-powered box that plugs into the radio's USB CAT port
  • An iPhone app talks to it over Bluetooth LE: frequency, mode, power, PTT, menus and memories
  • On the QMX it also streams I/Q, so the phone shows a live spectrum and waterfall
  • Open-source hardware, firmware, iOS apps and printable case
  • Built and operated by W2ASM

Supported radios

Yaesu FT-891
Full app: operate, meters, all 159 menu items, profiles. Yaesu CAT over the radio's CP2105 USB UART.
Yaesu FTX-1
Bridge working, app in progress. Same Yaesu CAT, reached through the radio's internal USB hub.
QRP Labs QMX
Full app: operate, live menu tree, panadapter, profiles. Kenwood TS-480 command subset over native USB CDC. Needs a USB-A host lead.

How it works

The bridge is a byte pipe. An ESP32-S3 runs a BLE GATT server on one side and a USB host (CDC and CP210x) on the other, and shuttles bytes between them. It knows nothing about CAT.

All the radio logic lives in the iOS app, in a Swift package called CATBridgeKit: CAT dialects, polling, state, menus, profiles and the panadapter DSP. Supporting a new radio is a Swift change, not a firmware change.

Because the link can key a transmitter, the pairing is bonded and encrypted, and the bridge drops PTT on its own if the connection goes quiet.

What the app does

Tune and operate
Frequency, mode, RF power and PTT with a dead-man failsafe. Drag the digits to spin the VFO, or tap to enter a frequency in kHz.
Panadapter
On the QMX, a real-time spectrum and waterfall from the radio's I/Q stream.
Menus
Browse the radio's whole configuration tree with a plain-English note on each item. The tree is discovered live, so it matches your firmware.
Profiles
Save the radio's full state to iCloud Drive, diff it against the live rig, and apply it back.

Building one

A unit is about a dozen parts, around 23 g of PETG, and an afternoon. You need a 3D printer, a temperature-controlled soldering iron, a multimeter, and a Mac with Xcode for the app. The full walkthrough is in BUILD.md; part numbers and substitutions are in BOM.md.

You also need a USB-to-UART dongle for flashing. The USB-C port runs in host mode for the radio, so the console and bootloader are on UART0.

Wiring

Seven runs. The switch sits upstream of both the board and the boost converter, so switching off also kills the boost. That keeps its 5 V from back-feeding a PC's USB port when you plug in to reflash.

  • Cell + → switch common
  • Switch out → XIAO BAT+
  • Switch out → boost Vin
  • Cell − → XIAO BAT−
  • Cell − → boost GND
  • Boost 5 V → XIAO VUSB
  • Boost GND → XIAO GND

Two things to be careful about. Cut the LiPo's JST plug off one lead at a time; cutting both together shorts the cell through your cutters. And measure the boost output before it touches the microcontroller: switch on, confirm 5 V on a meter, and only then solder it to VUSB.

Steps

  1. Print the case. Two parts, no supports: pocket_cat_base.stl and pocket_cat_lid.stl. PETG, 0.2 mm layers, 4 walls, 25% infill. The lid's skirt slides inside the base walls.
  2. Flash the firmware before assembly. The programming pads face into the case once it's built. Wire a USB-UART dongle to GPIO43/44, then idf.py set-target esp32s3 && idf.py flash. It should then advertise as CATBridge-XXXX in a BLE scanner.
  3. Solder the wiring. Seven runs, twisted in pairs to keep loop area down. Hot-glue the BAT joints; those pads lift. Switch on: the LED lights and it advertises. Switch off: all dark.
  4. Assemble the case. Antenna into its bay, switch on its pegs, board into the lid pocket connector-first, boost into its cradle. Lay the cell in loose, never glued, and leave the swelling gap clear. Close with the M3 from underneath.
  5. Build and install the app. Pick the app for your radio, set your signing team, run xcodegen generate, and build to your iPhone. A built-in simulator runs the whole app with no hardware.
  6. First contact. Plug the bridge into the radio's CAT port, power both on, and open the app. It pairs, probes the baud rate, identifies the rig, and goes live.
